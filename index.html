The Geometry of Motion

Understanding Circular Motion, Rotating Coordinates, and Kinematics

An Interactive Guide

A Quick Guide to Our Math Symbols

  • \(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{a}\) : Position, velocity, acceleration (vectors).
  • \(r, v, a\) : Scalar magnitudes of those vectors.
  • \(\hat{i}, \hat{j}\) : Standard, fixed (x, y) basis vectors.
  • \(\mathbf{u}_r, \mathbf{u}_\theta\) : Rotating (polar) basis vectors.
  • \(\omega, \alpha\) : Angular velocity and acceleration.
  • \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) : Angular velocity written as a 3D vector.

Preface

Throughout history, physics has balanced two ways of thinking about space. On Earth, when we build houses or survey land, we use straight lines and square grids. But thousands of years ago, before we mapped the land, humans mapped the sky. We tracked the moon, watched the sun, and observed the stars spinning above us. Because of this, humans realized very early on that natural motion is often circular.

This guide explores a big leap in introductory physics: moving from fixed, straight grids to coordinate systems that spin right along with the object you are studying. When we study circular motion, we run into a fascinating mathematical puzzle: how do you describe a spatial direction that is constantly changing?

By using interactive simulations alongside standard math equations, we're going to break down how to handle rotating coordinate systems. This isn't just useful for spinning wheels—it's the stepping stone you need for understanding everything from planetary orbits to Einstein's general relativity.

❦

1. Straight Lines vs. Curved Paths

To understand why we need special math for rotation, let's compare it to moving in a straight line. When a particle moves at a constant speed in a straight line, its velocity never changes—both its speed and its direction stay exactly the same.

However, when a particle moves at a constant speed in a circle, something is always changing. Even though its distance from the center stays the same, and its speedometer reading never changes, its direction is constantly shifting. Because the direction changes, we can't just use simple numbers; we need to use vectors, and we need a way to mathematically talk about a "changing direction."

Motion vs. Forces
Right now, we are only looking at Kinematics—which is just the math of drawing the motion itself. We aren't worried yet about Dynamics, which is the actual physical forces (like gravity or a string) causing the object to spin. We are just mapping out the geometry.

2. Choosing the Right Coordinates

The standard Cartesian grid—the \((x,y)\) system you learn in algebra—uses fixed, straight axes. While it's great for straight lines, it makes drawing a circle surprisingly messy. If you want to describe a particle moving in a perfect circle of radius \(r\), the \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates have to constantly wave up and down like this:

\[ x(t) = r\cos(\theta(t)), \quad y(t) = r\sin(\theta(t)) \]

Moving in a circle is a simple physical idea (just stay the same distance from the center!), but the \((x,y)\) math makes it look complicated. The trick is to change our coordinate system to match the motion. If we use Polar coordinates \((r, \theta)\), we just keep track of the distance from the center (\(r\)) and the angle (\(\theta\)). For a circle, \(r\) is simply a constant number. Much easier!

❦

3. Rotating Basis Vectors

When we use vectors to do math, we rely on "basis vectors"—these are essentially our tiny measuring sticks for direction. In the standard \((x,y)\) system, our measuring sticks \(\hat{i}\) (right) and \(\hat{j}\) (up) are bolted to the background. They never move.

But when we use polar coordinates, our measuring sticks ride along with the particle itself:

  • \(\mathbf{u}_r\): The radial vector. It always points directly away from the center, right through the particle.
  • \(\mathbf{u}_\theta\): The tangential vector. It points 90 degrees ahead of the radial vector, showing which way the particle is swinging.

Because these vectors are attached to the particle, they have to rotate as the particle moves around the circle.

4. The Derivative of a Direction

Since \(\mathbf{u}_r\) and \(\mathbf{u}_\theta\) are spinning, they are changing over time. In calculus, that means they have a derivative. To find the derivative of \(\mathbf{u}_r\), let's look at how the vector changes over a tiny sliver of time \(\Delta t\), during which it rotates by a tiny angle \(\Delta\theta\).

The Math of a Changing Vector
If we take the new vector \(\mathbf{u}_r(t + \Delta t)\) and subtract the old vector \(\mathbf{u}_r(t)\), we get the difference, which we call \(\Delta\mathbf{u}_r\). Because both vectors have a length of exactly 1, they form a triangle. Using basic trigonometry, the length of the difference is:
\[ |\Delta\mathbf{u}_r| = 2\sin\left(\frac{\Delta\theta}{2}\right) \]
When the angle \(\Delta\theta\) becomes extremely small, \(\sin(x)\) is basically just \(x\), which means the length of our difference vector simplifies to just \(|\Delta\mathbf{u}_r| \approx \Delta\theta\).

What about the direction of this difference? As the angle gets smaller and smaller, the difference vector \(\Delta\mathbf{u}_r\) becomes perfectly perpendicular to \(\mathbf{u}_r\). It points exactly in the \(\mathbf{u}_\theta\) direction!

If we divide by our time step \(\Delta t\), we get our formal derivative. (Note: \(\omega\) is the angular velocity, which just means \(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\)):

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{u}_r}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta\mathbf{u}_r}{\Delta t} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \left( \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} \right) \mathbf{u}_\theta = \omega \mathbf{u}_\theta \]

We can use the exact same logic for the tangential vector \(\mathbf{u}_\theta\). Because it is rotated 90 degrees ahead, when it shifts, it actually points inward, directly opposite to the radial vector. That's why it gets a negative sign:

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{u}_\theta}{dt} = -\omega \mathbf{u}_r \]

5. Why Velocity Points Tangentially

Now that we know how our measuring sticks spin, finding the velocity of the particle is surprisingly easy. First, we write down where the particle is. Its position is just its distance \(r\) in the radial direction:

\[ \mathbf{r} = r\mathbf{u}_r \]

Velocity is just the derivative of position. We use the product rule from calculus. Since the particle is moving in a circle, its distance \(r\) never changes, so \(\frac{dr}{dt}\) is just 0.

\[ \mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \frac{dr}{dt}\mathbf{u}_r + r\frac{d\mathbf{u}_r}{dt} = 0 + r(\omega \mathbf{u}_\theta) \]

This gives us \(\mathbf{v} = r\omega\mathbf{u}_\theta\). This math perfectly matches our intuition: the velocity of an object moving in a circle points straight along the edge (tangentially), and its speed is \(r\omega\).

Straight Lines on Curves
This mathematical result shows that if you zoom in close enough to a curve at any specific moment, the particle is basically just trying to travel in a straight line tangent to the circle.

6. Acceleration: Speed vs. Direction

Acceleration is how fast velocity is changing. To find it, we take the derivative of our velocity equation \(\mathbf{v} = r\omega\mathbf{u}_\theta\) using the product rule again. This time, we find that acceleration comes from two completely different things:

\[ \mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{d(r\omega)}{dt}\mathbf{u}_\theta + (r\omega)\frac{d\mathbf{u}_\theta}{dt} \]

Let's swap in \(\alpha\) (angular acceleration) for \(\frac{d\omega}{dt}\), and plug in our spinning vector rule \(\frac{d\mathbf{u}_\theta}{dt} = -\omega\mathbf{u}_r\). We end up with the final equation for acceleration:

\[ \mathbf{a} = r\alpha\mathbf{u}_\theta - r\omega^2\mathbf{u}_r \]
This splits acceleration into two distinct jobs:
  • Tangential Acceleration (\(r\alpha\)): This pushes the particle forward or backward along the curve, acting like the gas pedal or brakes to change its speed.
  • Centripetal Acceleration (\(-r\omega^2\)): This points dead-center inward. It doesn't change the speed at all; its only job is to yank the particle sideways to keep bending its path into a circle.

The inward acceleration (\(-r\omega^2\)) is directly tied to the curve of the path. A tighter curve (smaller \(r\)) or a faster speed means you need a much harder inward pull to keep changing direction.

7. Using Cross Products for 3D

Everything we've done so far is in a flat 2D plane. To step up to 3D motion, physicists use the vector cross product. We take the spinning speed \(\omega\) and turn it into a 3D vector \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) that points straight up out of the spinning plane (using the right-hand rule).

There is a neat mathematical trick: taking the derivative of any spinning vector \(\mathbf{A}\) is the same as just crossing it with \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\):

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{A}}{dt} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{A} \]

If we apply this cross product to the position vector, we get the velocity: \(\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}\). This trick saves us from having to manually track our local basis vectors; the cross product math handles all the 3D geometry for us automatically.

8. The Magic of Complex Numbers

If we stay in 2D, there is another incredibly elegant way to do this using imaginary numbers. Using Euler's formula, we can represent a point on a circle as a complex number \(z\):

\[ z = r e^{i\theta} \]

The \(e^{i\theta}\) part perfectly replaces our radial vector \(\mathbf{u}_r\). Because taking derivatives of 'e' is very easy in calculus, we can find velocity just by using the chain rule (assuming uniform speed):

\[ v = \frac{dz}{dt} = r \left( i \frac{d\theta}{dt} e^{i\theta} \right) = i\omega r e^{i\theta} \]

In the math of complex numbers, multiplying by \(i\) means "rotate exactly 90 degrees counter-clockwise." So, the \(i\) in this formula automatically rotates our vector from pointing outward to pointing sideways (tangentially). It's built right in!

If we take the derivative one more time to find acceleration, we get an \(i^2\):

\[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} = i\omega r \left( i\omega e^{i\theta} \right) = i^2 \omega^2 r e^{i\theta} = -\omega^2 r e^{i\theta} \]

Since everyone knows that \(i^2 = -1\), the math magically spits out a negative sign, proving that the acceleration points directly inward towards the center. Complex numbers handle the rotation geometry effortlessly.

9. Rotating Frames & Apparent Forces

Why does all of this rotating vector math matter so much? Because we live on a rotating planet. Whenever you measure something from the surface of the Earth, you are standing inside a rotating coordinate system.

When you do math from the perspective of a spinning room, the changing vectors make it look like invisible forces are pushing objects sideways. These are called "fictitious" or "apparent" forces—the most famous being the Coriolis force. They aren't real pushes or pulls; they are just what happens when the grid you are standing on rotates beneath an object trying to move in a straight line.

Conclusion

The math behind moving in a circle is a huge stepping stone in physics. It forces us to stop using fixed grids and start using coordinates that adapt and spin along with the object we are measuring. By understanding how a simple unit vector rotates, we unlock the math needed to handle much bigger concepts.

Whether you use polar coordinates, cross products, or complex numbers, the big takeaway is the same: the coordinate space itself is dynamic. Once you are comfortable with geometry that changes and spins, you are ready to tackle everything from the chaotic spins of a gyroscope to the curving fabric of space-time.

Appendix: The Center of Mass

While this guide has focused on tracking a single particle, a similar shift in thinking happens when we look at multiple objects orbiting each other. Instead of measuring from a random static point, the math gets much easier if we measure everything from the system's natural balancing point: the center of mass (or barycenter).
